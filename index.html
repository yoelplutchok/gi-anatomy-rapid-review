Each part keeps its embryonic artery even after the gut rotates and moves. So an organ's division tells you its artery, nerves and pain site, and its artery tells you its division.

1Building the gut tube

  • In week 4 the flat embryo folds. The head fold makes the foregut. The tail fold makes the hindgut and its end chamber, the cloaca.
  • The middle part, the midgut, stays open to the yolk sac through a narrowing stalk, the vitelline duct.
  • The lateral body folds roll the sides inward. They close the gut into a tube and close the front body wall at the same time. If they fail to fuse, bowel lies outside the body: gastroschisis.
  • At the two ends, ectoderm lies directly on endoderm with no mesoderm between. These thin spots are the stomodeum (future mouth) and the proctodeum (future anus); their membranes later break open.

A mesentery is a double layer of peritoneum that ties the gut to a body wall. The gut's vessels and nerves reach it through the mesentery.

  • The dorsal mesentery runs along the whole gut and ties it to the back wall. It becomes the greater omentum, the gastrosplenic and splenorenal ligaments, the mesentery proper and the mesocolons.
  • The ventral mesentery exists along the foregut only and ties it to the front wall. It becomes the lesser omentum and the falciform ligament.

2Foregut: esophagus to duodenum

Plug and reopen

  • The tracheoesophageal septum splits the front of the foregut off as the trachea. The trachea lies in front and the esophagus behind.
  • In week 5 the lining grows so fast that it plugs the esophagus and the duodenum. Small holes then open up by cell death and merge, so the tube is open again (recanalization) by week 8.
  • If the tube never reopens, the result is atresia (no lumen at all). If it reopens only partly, the result is stenosis (a narrow lumen).

Esophageal atresia with or without TEF

  • The esophagus ends in a blind upper pouch. The cause is a septum that deviates backward, or a lumen that never reopens.
  • Before birth the mother has polyhydramnios. After birth the baby drools, because saliva cannot pass the pouch, and chokes and turns blue at the first feed. A feeding tube coils in the pouch.
  • The most common type (~85%) is a blind upper pouch with the lower esophagus joined to the trachea. Air reaches the stomach through that fistula, so the stomach contains air.
  • In pure atresia (no fistula) no air can reach the stomach, so the belly is gasless and flat.
  • It often comes with other defects in the VACTERL cluster: vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal and limb.

Amniotic fluid

  • The fetus swallows amniotic fluid and absorbs it in its gut. Its kidneys add fluid back as urine. The volume is the balance of the two.
  • Polyhydramnios (too much fluid) means the fetus cannot swallow it down, as in esophageal or duodenal atresia. Low blocks (anus, colon) do not cause it, because the fluid is already absorbed higher up.
  • Oligohydramnios (too little fluid) means no urine is made, as in bilateral renal agenesis. The cramped fetus gets a flat face, limb defects and small lungs (Potter sequence).

Stomach rotation

  1. The stomach's dorsal wall grows faster than its ventral wall. The dorsal wall becomes the greater curvature, and the ventral wall becomes the lesser curvature.
  2. The stomach then rotates 90° to the left around its long axis (clockwise when seen from above). Its left side now faces the front, which is why the left vagus ends up as the anterior vagal trunk.
  3. The rotation pulls the dorsal mesogastrium to the left. It keeps growing, folds on itself, and hangs down as the greater omentum (4 layers). The space left behind the stomach is the omental bursa (lesser sac).
  4. The duodenum swings into a C-shape on the right. It is pressed onto the back wall and its mesentery fuses there, so it becomes secondarily retroperitoneal.

Spleen: it starts in week 5 as mesoderm between the two layers of the dorsal mesogastrium, behind the stomach. It is not made from gut endoderm. The stomach's rotation carries it to the left upper abdomen. Before birth it is a blood-forming organ.

Vomiting newborn

Bile enters the duodenum at the major papilla. If the block is above the papilla, the vomit has no bile (non-bilious). If the block is below it, the vomit is green (bilious).

3Liver and pancreas

  • The hepatic diverticulum (liver bud) grows out of the ventral wall of the lowest foregut, the future duodenum. It grows forward into the septum transversum, a plate of mesoderm.
  • Its upper part becomes the liver cells and the bile capillaries. Its lower (cystic) part becomes the gallbladder and cystic duct. Its stalk becomes the bile duct.
  • So the liver cells are endoderm, but the liver's fibrous tissue and its Kupffer cells (macrophages) are mesoderm from the septum transversum.
  • The growing liver splits the ventral mesentery in two. The piece between the liver and the stomach and duodenum becomes the lesser omentum; its free edge carries the portal triad. The piece between the liver and the front wall becomes the falciform ligament.
  • After birth the umbilical vein (in the falciform free edge) closes and becomes the ligamentum teres. The ductus venosus closes and becomes the ligamentum venosum.

  • Annular pancreas: the ventral bud has two lobes. During rotation one passes behind the duodenum and one in front, and they meet as a ring around D2 that can block it.
  • Pancreas divisum: the two ducts never fuse, so most of the gland drains through the small accessory duct.

4Midgut rotation

The midgut forms a U-shaped primary intestinal loop. The SMA runs down its middle, so the SMA is both its blood supply and the axis it turns around. The loop's tip is tied to the yolk sac by the vitelline duct.

  • The cranial (upper) arm becomes most of the small intestine. It grows fast and coils.
  • The caudal (lower) arm becomes the lower ileum, cecum, appendix, ascending colon and proximal 2/3 of the transverse colon.
  1. Week 6: the large liver and kidneys leave too little room, so the loop herniates into the umbilical cord. This is normal (physiologic herniation). In the cord it turns 90° counterclockwise, seen from the front.
  2. Week 10: the loop returns to the abdomen and turns 180° more, so the total is 270° counterclockwise. The small bowel goes back first and the cecum last; the cecum then drops from the right upper to the right lower abdomen. The appendix is a pouch that grows off the cecum.
  3. The ascending and descending colon press onto the back wall and their mesenteries fuse (secondarily retroperitoneal). The cecum, appendix and sigmoid keep their mesenteries.

Normal result: the cecum lies in the right lower quadrant, and the transverse colon passes in front of the SMA.

The one feature that separates the first two is the sac: an omphalocele has one, gastroschisis does not.

Meckel diverticulum is the leftover gut end of the vitelline duct. It sits on the ileum, on the side away from the mesentery. Rule of 2s: about 2% of people, 2 inches long, 2 feet from the ileocecal junction.

  • It can contain stomach tissue. That tissue makes acid, the acid ulcerates the ileum next to it, and a child has painless rectal bleeding. A technetium scan finds the stomach tissue.
  • If the whole duct stays open (vitelline fistula), stool leaks from the umbilicus.
  • If the duct stays as a fibrous cord to the umbilicus, bowel can twist around the cord and become blocked.

6Hindgut and anal canal

  1. Week 4: the tail fold makes the cloaca, one chamber that the gut and the urinary tract share. The allantois leaves its top and becomes the urachus (adult median umbilical ligament).
  2. Weeks 4–7: a wedge of mesoderm, the urorectal septum, grows down and splits the cloaca. The front part is the urogenital sinus (bladder, urethra). The back part is the rectum and upper anal canal.
  3. End of week 7–8: the anal membrane ruptures, so the ectoderm pit (proctodeum) opens into the hindgut. The pectinate line marks where that membrane was.

The anal canal has two origins, and the pectinate line is the seam. Above it everything is gut-like (visceral). Below it everything is skin-like (somatic).

Hirschsprung disease: neural crest cells migrate down the gut from top to bottom to build its nerve plexuses. Here they stop short, so the rectum and sigmoid have no ganglion cells (the postganglionic parasympathetic cell bodies of the Meissner and Auerbach plexuses). Without them the segment cannot relax. Stool backs up and the colon above it swells (megacolon).

Clue: no meconium, a swollen belly, a normal anus but a tight canal, and a gush of stool when the examining finger is pulled out. A biopsy shows no ganglion cells.

7Testis descent

  • The testis forms high on the back wall, near the kidney. That is why its artery comes from the aorta and its lymph drains to the para-aortic nodes, not to the groin nodes.
  • The gubernaculum, a cord from the testis to the scrotum, guides it down. A finger of peritoneum, the processus vaginalis, goes ahead of it. The tunnel they make through the wall is the inguinal canal.
  • In females the gubernaculum becomes the ovarian ligament and the round ligament of the uterus.
  • If the processus stays open, bowel can slide into it: a congenital indirect inguinal hernia. If only fluid collects in it, the result is a hydrocele. If the testis stops on its way down, the result is cryptorchidism, which raises the cancer risk.

The transpyloric plane (L1) lies halfway between the jugular notch and the pubic symphysis. Many structures sit on it: the pylorus, gallbladder fundus, pancreatic neck, SMA origin, start of the portal vein, duodenojejunal junction, both renal hila and the 9th costal cartilages.   • The spleen lies under left ribs 9–11. It is the organ most often torn in blunt trauma.

  • The lateral fold (inferior epigastric vessels) is the landmark that sorts inguinal hernias. Medial to it is Hesselbach triangle, where direct hernias push through. Lateral to it is the deep ring, where indirect hernias enter.

The canal

The testis pushed through the wall, led by a pouch of peritoneum (the processus vaginalis) and guided by the gubernaculum. The path it left is the canal: a slanted tunnel about 4 cm long, just above the medial half of the inguinal ligament. Because it slants, its two rings do not line up, which helps keep it shut when belly pressure rises.

6Spermatic cord and scrotum

Cord contents (everything running to and from the testis):

  • the ductus deferens;
  • 3 arteries: testicular (from the aorta), cremasteric (from the inferior epigastric) and the artery of the ductus (from a vesical artery);
  • the pampiniform venous plexus and lymph vessels;
  • the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve and sympathetic fibers;
  • the thread left by the processus vaginalis.

7Testis and epididymis

  • The testis forms high on the back wall, beside the kidneys, and drags its vessels down with it. That is why the testicular artery comes from the aorta (just below the renal arteries).

  • Sperm route: seminiferous tubules → rete testis → efferent ductules → head of the epididymis.
  • The epididymis sits on the back of the testis. It stores sperm and lets them mature. The ductus deferens starts at its tail, runs up in the cord, through the deep ring and into the pelvis. 1Peritoneum

  • Parietal peritoneum lines the wall. It has somatic nerves, like the wall itself, so its pain is sharp and well localized. It comes from somatic mesoderm.
  • Visceral peritoneum covers the organs. It has only visceral (autonomic) sensory nerves, so its pain is dull and poorly localized. It comes from splanchnic mesoderm. Endoderm and ectoderm make no peritoneum.
  • The peritoneal cavity is a potential space with only a film of fluid. No organ and no lymph node lies inside it. It is closed in males but open in females, through the tubes, uterus and vagina.
  • An intraperitoneal organ is wrapped almost all round and hangs on a mesentery, so it can move. It is still outside the cavity, like a fist pushed into a balloon.
  • A retroperitoneal organ lies behind the peritoneum and is covered only in front. It has no mesentery, so it is fixed.

2Ligaments, omenta, mesenteries

All are double layers of peritoneum. A ligament joins an organ to an organ or the wall, an omentum hangs from the stomach or D1, and a mesentery ties intestine to the back wall. The ventral mesentery made the lesser omentum and falciform lig.; the dorsal mesentery made the rest.

  • The lesser omentum joins the liver to the lesser curvature and D1. Its thin left part, the hepatogastric lig., carries the gastric vessels. Its thick right part, the hepatoduodenal lig., has a free edge that carries the portal triad.
  • The greater omentum hangs from the greater curvature as a 4-layer apron over the intestines. It is the gastrophrenic, gastrosplenic (carries the short gastrics + left gastro-omental) and gastrocolic ligs. No liver ligament is part of it.
  • The splenorenal lig. joins the spleen to the left kidney. It carries the splenic a. + v. and the pancreatic tail.
  • The falciform lig. joins the liver to the front wall and diaphragm. Its free edge holds the round lig., the old left umbilical vein.
  • The mesentery root (~15 cm) runs from the duodenojejunal junction (left of L2) to the right sacroiliac joint.

3Portal triad and epiploic foramen

The hepatic veins and cystic duct are not in it. In the Pringle maneuver the surgeon squeezes this edge, which stops all blood flowing into the liver.

7Duodenum (D1) and GDA

  • A C-loop around the pancreatic head; only the first 2 cm are intraperitoneal. Behind D1: GDA, bile duct, portal v., IVC. In front of D1: liver, gallbladder.

8Veins, lymph, nerves

  • The left and right gastric veins drain straight into the portal v. The short gastric and left gastro-omental veins drain into the splenic v., and the right gastro-omental into the SMV.
  • Lymph follows the arteries to the celiac nodes, then the cisterna chyli (a lymph sac, not a node, right of the aorta behind the right crus), then the thoracic duct.
  • Parasympathetic: the vagal trunks (anterior = left vagus, posterior = right). They speed peristalsis and relax the pylorus, and pass the celiac plexus without synapsing.
  • Sympathetic: the greater splanchnic nerve (T5–T9), which synapses in the celiac ganglion. It slows peristalsis and closes the pylorus.
  • Foregut pain is felt in the epigastrium (pancreas: also the back), because its pain fibers enter the cord at T5–T9. Vagal fibers carry fullness, not pain. Cutting the vagus lowers acid.

3SMA

  • It leaves the aorta at L1, about 1 cm below the celiac trunk, and runs down to the right in the mesentery root. The SMV runs on its right.
  • In front of the SMA: the splenic v. and the neck of the pancreas. Behind it, from top to bottom: the left renal v., the uncinate process and D3.
  • The SMA–aorta angle can pinch what passes through it. SMA syndrome: big weight loss removes the fat pad, so D3 is crushed (early fullness, vomiting).   • Branches, top to bottom: inferior pancreaticoduodenal → middle colic → 15–18 jejunal and ileal (to the left) → right colic → ileocolic (the end branch).
  • If the SMA is blocked, the jejunum and ileum die first, because no other artery reaches them. Slow narrowing causes intestinal angina: pain after meals, fear of eating, weight loss.

Aortic levels: celiac T12 · SMA L1 · renal L1–L2 · gonadal L2 · IMA L3 · split into iliacs L4.

4Large intestine

Three muscle bands, the teniae coli, gather the wall into pouches (haustra), and fat tags (epiploic appendages) hang off it. The appendix and rectum have no teniae.

  • The ascending and descending colon are secondarily retroperitoneal, so they are fixed. The cecum is intraperitoneal but has no mesentery. The rectum has none either.
  • The appendix (mesoappendix), transverse colon (most mobile) and sigmoid hang on mesenteries. The sigmoid's is long, so it can twist (volvulus).
  • The left ureter lies behind the apex of the sigmoid mesocolon, where the left common iliac a. divides, so it is at risk in sigmoid surgery.
  • The ileocecal valve does not stop reflux. The terminal ileum is the narrowest part of the small bowel, so blocks happen here: intussusception in a child, a gallstone in an adult.
  • The appendix base is on the back-inner side of the cecum. Usually retrocecal (~64%), else pelvic (~32%). McBurney point, one-third of the way from the right ASIS to the umbilicus, lies over its base.
  • Appendicitis pain starts around the umbilicus, because visceral nerves (T10) give only a vague midline ache. It moves to the RLQ when the parietal peritoneum (sharp somatic nerves) gets irritated.
  • Crohn: mouth to anus, often terminal ileum (RLQ). Ulcerative colitis: rectum upward, no gaps (LLQ). Diverticulosis: painless sigmoid pouches; inflamed = diverticulitis (LLQ pain, fever).
  • Hirschsprung: neural crest cells never reach the lower colon, so it has no myenteric ganglion cells and cannot relax. Stool backs up and the colon above swells (megacolon).

6Collaterals

The three gut arteries link up where their territories meet, so one blocked artery can be bypassed.

  • Celiac to SMA: the superior pancreaticoduodenal (from the GDA) meets the inferior pancreaticoduodenal (from the SMA) on the pancreatic head.
  • SMA to IMA: the marginal artery (of Drummond) runs along the inner edge of the whole colon and is almost always present. The arc of Riolan is a shorter loop near the mesentery root that joins the middle colic to the left colic; it is often absent.
  • If the IMA is blocked, blood runs from the middle colic through the marginal artery into the left colic territory. So an IMA blockage alone is often silent. If the SMA is blocked instead, the same chain runs backward and the IMA fills the transverse colon.
  • The splenic flexure sits at the far ends of both the SMA and IMA trees, where collateral flow is thinnest. It is the watershed most prone to ischemic colitis.

7Lymph and nerves

  • Lymph follows the arteries back to the aorta. Absorbed fat enters lacteals and ends in the cisterna chyli.
  • Colon lymph passes four node groups in order: epicolic (on the wall), paracolic (along the marginal a.), intermediate colic, then the main SMA or IMA nodes. Cancer spreads in the same order. The cecum and appendix drain to the ileocolic nodes.
  • Nerve plexuses ride on the arteries, so they split the same way. Sympathetic fibers slow the gut and synapse in the prevertebral ganglion of their region: celiac (foregut), superior mesenteric (midgut), inferior mesenteric (hindgut). Parasympathetic fibers speed the gut and synapse in its wall. The pelvic splanchnics (S2–S4) are the only parasympathetic ones.

1The two-neuron plan

Somatic (voluntary): 1 neuron, anterior horn to skeletal muscle. Autonomic (involuntary): 2 neurons in a row to smooth muscle, heart muscle or glands.

  • The pre neuron's cell body sits in CNS gray matter (it comes from the neural tube). Its axon is myelinated and ends in a ganglion.
  • The post neuron's cell body sits in that ganglion (it comes from neural crest). Its axon is unmyelinated and ends on the target.
  • So an autonomic cell body in the CNS is pre; one in a ganglion is post. Dorsal root ganglia hold sensory cell bodies (no synapse).

2Sympathetic pathway

  • Every sympathetic pre cell body sits in the lateral horn (intermediolateral column), which exists only at T1–L2. The cervical and sacral cord and the brainstem send out no sympathetic fibers.
  • "White in, gray out": the axon leaves in the ventral root, then enters the chain through the white ramus. This ramus is white because its fibers are myelinated, and it exists only at T1–L2.
  • Post fibers return to the spinal nerve through the gray ramus (unmyelinated). Every spinal nerve has one, so sweat glands and vessels everywhere get fibers.
  • In the chain, a pre fiber can: synapse at its own level; climb and then synapse (for the head, since outflow starts at T1); descend and then synapse (for the pelvis and legs, since it stops at L2); or pass through without synapsing as a splanchnic nerve to a prevertebral ganglion (for the abdominal organs).
  • The chain runs from the superior cervical ganglion (highest, below the skull) to the ganglion impar (coccyx).

Segments: head, neck, arm T1–T4 · thorax T1–T5 · abdomen T5–T12 · pelvis T11–L2 · leg T12–L2. So an arm that cannot sweat means a lateral horn T1–T4 lesion (a dorsal root lesion only removes feeling).

3Head sympathetics and Horner

Pre cell bodies in the lateral horn of T1–T2 leave through the ventral roots and white rami. They climb the chain past the stellate ganglion (at the lung apex) and synapse in the superior cervical ganglion. The post fibers wrap the carotid arteries (carotid plexuses) and ride their branches to 3 targets: facial sweat glands, the dilator pupillae, and the superior tarsal muscle, which helps hold the upper lid up.

4Plexuses and visceral pain

  • A splanchnic nerve's name does not tell its fiber type. Pelvic = the only parasympathetic one; sacral = sympathetic; cardiopulmonary = post (already synapsed in the chain).
  • Post fibers from the prevertebral ganglia form a plexus on each aortic branch and ride that artery to its organ. Plexus levels: celiac T12 · superior mesenteric L1 · renal L1–L2 · inferior mesenteric L3.
  • The superior hypogastric plexus (below the aortic bifurcation, in front of L5) splits into right and left hypogastric nerves (post sympathetic).
  • The inferior hypogastric plexus is mixed: hypogastric nerves + sacral splanchnics (sympathetic) and pelvic splanchnics (parasympathetic).

Visceral pain runs the sympathetic route backward. Its fibers enter the dorsal root ganglia of the organ's own segments, so the brain feels the pain in the skin of those segments. That is why it is dull, poorly localized and midline.

4Heart on CT and angiogram

  • The right ventricle is the most anterior chamber, right behind the sternum. The right atrium forms the right heart edge (image left).
  • The left atrium is the most posterior chamber, directly in front of the esophagus at T7. So a big left atrium pushes the esophagus back.
  • The left ventricle has the thick wall and forms the apex; on a ventriculogram it is the lower-left chamber with the aorta rising from it.
  • Left coronary angiogram: the left main splits into the LAD (left anterior descending: front groove, gives diagonals, the "widow maker") and the circumflex (gives marginals).

5Plain abdominal film

  • Gastric bubble: LUQ, under the left hemidiaphragm. Splenic flexure: high in the LUQ; hepatic flexure under the liver.
  • Jejunum (left upper): feathery folds across the full width. Ileum (RLQ, pelvis): fewer folds. Colon: haustra (folds across only part of the width) + stool.
  • Small bowel obstruction: central loops wider than 3 cm, an abrupt transition point, and a collapsed colon beyond it.
  • Free air rises, so it is best seen on an erect film as a dark crescent under the right hemidiaphragm. On a supine film look for the Rigler sign (both sides of the bowel wall visible) or the football sign (big oval of air; the falciform ligament is the lace).
  • Chilaiditi: hepatic flexure between liver and diaphragm; a normal variant, not free air (its haustra show it is colon).

6Barium studies

  • Fluoroscopy = live X-ray; barium makes the lumen white. Lateral esophagram: hyoid + epiglottis on top; esophagus behind trachea and heart.
  • Stomach: fundus = dome above the esophageal entry, under the left hemidiaphragm. The antrum narrows to the right and ends at the pylorus.
  • The duodenal C-loop wraps the pancreatic head (L1–L3). D1 is the bulb. D2 runs down right of the spine and holds the major papilla. D3 crosses between the SMA and the aorta. D4 rises on the left to the jejunum.

7Abdominal CT, US, MRI

  • IVC: right of the spine, in a groove on the back of the liver. Aorta: just left of midline, hugged by the crura.
  • Caudate lobe: between portal vein and IVC. Right adrenal: thin line behind the IVC. Falciform ligament: fat cleft between the lobes.
  • Stomach: find its air bubble. Spleen: far left and back, at ribs 9–11; the pancreatic tail points to it.
  • Kidneys lie at T12–L3. The left renal vein crosses in front of the aorta; the right renal vein is short and enters the IVC directly.
  • Kidney phases: corticomedullary (cortex bright, pyramids dark), nephrographic (even), excretory (contrast in the collecting system). Horseshoe: lower poles fused. Hydronephrosis: swollen pelvis + calyces behind a block.
  • Liver segment: portal triad in the center, hepatic veins at the edges. The caudate drains straight into the IVC, so it is spared in early cirrhosis.
  • Pancreas: head in the C-loop, uncinate behind the SMV, body over the splenic vein + aorta, tail at the splenic hilum. Pancreatitis: swollen gland, stranded fat, fluid.
  • The SMV (vertical) and splenic vein (horizontal) join behind the pancreatic neck to form the portal vein.

8Mesenteric angiograms

  • SMA branch order: inferior pancreaticoduodenal, middle colic (1st big right branch), jejunal/ileal (fan left), right colic, ileocolic (lowest; gives the appendicular a.).
